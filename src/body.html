The simulation

The forecast plays out the election thousands of times, each with a slightly different party vote and electorate results, and counts how often each outcome happens . The cards below are the factors that decide who can govern. Each bar shows how much a factor moves the odds; open a card to see why it matters and to change it.

How much it moves the odds

Under MMP the party vote sets how many seats a party gets. Electorate winners fill those seats first and list MPs fill the rest. If a party wins more electorates than its party vote earns, it keeps them all and Parliament grows to fit them. Those are overhang seats, and each one raises the number needed for a majority.

    Why this forecast's numbers differ from other forecasts

    Other New Zealand forecasts, such as Kiwi Polls, the Herald–Motu Poll of Polls, election2026.nz and Poliwave, often give different odds. That's mostly down to a handful of modelling choices, not one of them being wrong. Here's each one, and how much it moves the numbers on your current settings.

    1. It starts from one poll, not an average

    Most other forecasts start from an average of recent polls. This one starts from whichever poll is picked at the top, the latest by default, so a single poll that moves a few points moves the whole page. Choose the poll average in the poll menu to compare like with like.

    2. It doesn't correct for each pollster's lean

    Some pollsters run a little high or low for particular parties. The Herald–Motu Poll of Polls and election2026.nz adjust for that using past elections; this forecast takes every poll as published. That explains some of the gap, especially for smaller parties.

    3. It counts overhang seats

    When a party wins more electorates than its party vote earns, it keeps them and Parliament grows past 120, which raises the number of seats needed to govern. The Herald–Motu Poll of Polls assumes a 120-seat House, which helps whichever side doesn't benefit from overhang.

    4. Kingmakers aren't counted on either side

    A "National-led" or "Labour-led" majority here means that side's traditional bloc alone has a majority. Forecasts that report named coalitions, such as the Herald–Motu Poll of Polls, put those parties with one side, which can move the odds a long way. You can change any party's side in the blocs section above.

    5. Every electorate is simulated candidate by candidate

    Each of the 71 seats is decided in every simulated election, starting from 2023 results and the published electorate polls linked on each seat card, then nudged by the party vote. Poliwave and others apply a uniform swing from the last election instead. On recent polls National is close to winning more electorates than its party vote earns, and Te Pāti Māori usually does, so single electorates can change the size of the House and who governs.

    6. The size of polling misses is fixed

    This forecast assumes a typical New Zealand polling error unless you change it. election2026.nz widens its uncertainty the further away the election is.

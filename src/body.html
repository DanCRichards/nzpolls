The simulation

A party needs 5% of the party vote or an electorate to get any list seats. Under 5%, Opportunity's MPs vanish and their seats are shared among everyone else, which is why its vote and its side decide so much.

How much it moves the odds

Under MMP the party vote sets how many seats a party gets. Electorate winners fill those seats first and list MPs fill the rest. If a party wins more electorates than its party vote earns, it keeps them all and Parliament grows to fit them. Those are overhang seats, and each one raises the number needed for a majority.

How much it moves the odds

Hover over each option for what it means. Polls usually miss the final result by a few points, and misses often favour one side: in some elections the National-led parties beat their polling, in others the Labour-led parties do. Each simulation draws its own miss, so this setting controls how wide the range of outcomes is.

"Off" turns the simulations off: you get one result using the poll exactly as published, with every electorate going to its favourite, and every chance shows as 0% or 100%.

Te Pāti Māori's overhang
How much it moves the odds

Te Pāti Māori is campaigning only for electorate votes, and its list has one name. Its party vote is worth one or two seats, so each Māori electorate it holds beyond that adds a seat to the House and raises the bar for a majority. Call these seats to test it.

Breakaway MPs in Māori seats
How much it moves the odds

Mariameno Kapa-Kingi (Te Tai Tokerau Party) and Tākuta Ferris (independent) both left Te Pāti Māori. A win for either adds a seat to the House, counted here as "Independent / other". Neither has committed to a side: Kapa-Kingi has said she'd consider working with National (1News), and Ferris urges a party vote for the left but would work with the current coalition (The Spinoff), so they sit with the kingmakers unless you move them.
